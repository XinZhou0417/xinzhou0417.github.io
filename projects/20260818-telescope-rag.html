Great Melbourne Telescope Restoration

## Background

The Great Melbourne Telescope was built in 1869. It was the largest fully steerable telescope of its age and the first great reflector to use a clockwork-driven equatorial mount for automatic tracking. During more than a century's service, it helped produce the first systematic survey of the southern nebulae, discovering more than sixty objects unrecorded in any existing catalogue.

However, a bushfire in 2003 brought an end to its service. The bushfire melted the main frames, the delicate internal mechanisms, and everything modern around it, leaving only the 19th century's heavy ironworks.

In 2008, the Astronomical Society of Victoria, Museums Victoria and the Royal Botanic Gardens Melbourne started the restoration project to rebuild the Great Melbourne Telescope. The engineering work began in late 2013, after weighing different proposals for years. Since then, under the coordination of Museums Victoria, the university partners including Swinburne, Monash and the University of Melbourne have contributed engineering students to the restoration. And that's what linked me to this project.

## Internship

I joined the Great Melbourne Telescope Restoration project (GMTR) in late November 2024. By the time the software job was handed over to me, it was already a large, sophisticated project, with a heap of documentation.

During the internship, my main task was to help build the web controlling interface for the supporting control console. The funny part is that I built a collision detection algorithm to help the operator of the telescope determine the safe angle for the telescope to rotate. It is a combination of 3D point cloud distance calculation, linear algebra, and computer graphics. So, the workflow is: I import the 3D model to the interface, and it starts to detect the "safezone" in a 2D conceptualised space (representing the settings of the two axes of the telescope). After a full sweep, the "edge" of the safezone is recorded as discrete points, then mapped to an equatorial coordinate system that can be visualised in astronomy software.

## Volunteer

After finishing the internship, I continued as a volunteer for around half a year. During that period, I gradually adopted "agentic engineering" (a.k.a. vibe coding) into my daily workflow. At that time (early 2025), Cursor, the main vibe coding tool, was still not so good at working on a very large and legacy software project like this one. And this drove me to the idea of integrating a RAG into the system.

That idea came from the headache of finding relevant information among the huge amount of documentation and technical records (over 200, spanning almost a decade). As a software guy, I really don't understand the hardware part, but what I've built, the control interface, relies highly on the data input from the hardware, and I barely know where to find the configurations. Every time this happened, I had to consult the hardware team for support, who were not always available.

So, I decided to build a small hardware consultant of my own, based on the existing documentation and the code. The documentation contributed a lot to that knowledge base. I finally built a textbook RAG, with basic semantic chunking and embedding retrieval. I later made it a service that can be accessed by the software team, packed in an MCP server.
